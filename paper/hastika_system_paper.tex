\begin{abstract}
We describe our systems for the two HASTIKA-ICON-2026 tasks on Kannada--English
YouTube comments: hate-speech detection (\taska{}) and six-way categorization of
hateful comments (\taskb{}). Most of the Kannada in these comments is typed in
Latin script, with no fixed spelling. We tested character n-gram SVMs, MuRIL with
task-adaptive pretraining (TAPT), translation into English, and four decoder
language models. Our systems fine-tune Gemma-4-12B, quantized to 4 bits to fit a
single 16\,GB T4 GPU, with LoRA adapters and a linear head on the last
prompt token. On a held-out 15\% of the labelled data, two such models score
0.8610 macro-F1 on \taska{} (character n-gram SVM: 0.8117) and 0.6872 on \taskb{}
(best MuRIL system: 0.6151). Adding TAPT and averaging models from several TAPT
runs reached 0.8721 and 0.7056. The submitted systems average 12 and 8 such
models trained on all labelled data; the organizers report 0.8250 and 0.7367
macro-F1 for them on the official test sets. Code, recipes and a reproduction
package are available at
\url{https://github.com/Aaryan127/ProjectMANAS-HastikaICON2026}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
HASTIKA-ICON-2026 \citep{hastika-2026-starting-kit} asks participants to
classify Kannada--English YouTube comments. \taska{} labels a comment Hate or Non-Hate. \taskb{} assigns each
hateful comment to one of six categories: Gender, Geo-political, Others,
Political, Religion or Violence. Most Kannada in the comments is typed in Latin
script, and the spelling varies from writer to writer: ``don't know'' appears as
\textit{gottilla}, \textit{gothila}, \textit{gothilla}, \textit{gotila} and
\textit{gotilla}. A word-level model sees five unrelated features, and a subword
tokenizer may split each spelling into different pieces. Representing romanized
Kannada is therefore the central difficulty of both tasks. Hate-speech
detection is well studied in English \citep{davidson-etal-2017-automated,
fortuna-nunes-2018-survey} and in code-mixed text \citep{bohra-etal-2018-dataset},
including Kannada--English \citep{hande-etal-2020-kancmd,
chakravarthi-etal-2021-findings-shared, chakravarthi-etal-2022-dravidiancodemix}.

One technique runs through the paper. \emph{Task-adaptive pretraining} (TAPT;
\citealp{gururangan-etal-2020-dont}) continues a pretrained model's own training
objective on the unlabelled text of the task before it sees any labels:
masked-token prediction for an encoder such as MuRIL, next-token prediction for a
decoder such as Gemma. A model pretrained mostly on standard text has seen little
Kannada written as these commenters write it; TAPT adapts it to that register
before supervised training.

\paragraph{\taska{}.} A TF-IDF SVM performed at least as well as a fine-tuned
MuRIL \citep{khanuja-etal-2021-muril}, a model pretrained on Indian languages
including transliterated text, and remained a component of every \taska{} system
before the move to decoder models. A tuned MuRIL blended with the SVM reached
0.8188 in the validation phase. Translation into English removed most of the
abusive content and was abandoned. The largest improvement came from a decoder
language model, Gemma-4-12B \citep{gemma-team-2026-gemma4}, fine-tuned in 4 bits:
0.043 above the SVM on our holdout, and 0.8721 with TAPT ensembles.

\paragraph{\taskb{}.} Error analysis showed that most \taskb{} errors depend on
\emph{who} a comment targets. Of the changes we
evaluated on MuRIL, three helped: TAPT, re-initializing one top layer, and R-Drop.
Together they were our best system before Gemma (0.6410 in the validation
phase). Gemma then gained 0.064 over that recipe on the holdout, and TAPT
ensembles of Gemma reached 0.7056.

Our submissions average Gemma classifiers trained after several independent
TAPT runs: 12 for \taska{} and 8 for \taskb{}. Appendix~\ref{app:timeline}
summarizes the development history, including negative results.

\section{Task and Data}
\label{sec:data}
The comments come from the HASTIKA corpus \citep{kavatagi-rachh-2025}, released
for the shared task \citep{hastika-2026-starting-kit}, and also contain English, some Kannada script, emoji, URLs, user mentions and elongated
words. The organizers released a training set and, later, the labels of the
validation set. For each task we merged the two and removed duplicates
(Section~\ref{sec:prep}), leaving 7,193 of 7,252 comments for \taska{} (3,663
Non-Hate, 3,530 Hate) and 3,532 of 3,554 for \taskb{} (Gender 1,522, Political
624, Others 502, Religion 429, Violence 248, Geo-political 207). From each merged
set we drew one stratified 15\% holdout with scikit-learn
\citep{pedregosa-etal-2011-scikit}, 1,079 comments for \taska{} and 530 for
\taskb{}; the \taskb{} holdout has only 31 Geo-political and 37 Violence
comments. The test sets contain 806 and 396 comments; the final evaluation
scored only the final portion of each.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
We report macro-F1, which gives Geo-political (6\% of \taskb{}) the same weight
as Gender (43\%). Before the validation labels were released, we compared systems
by five-fold cross-validation on the deduplicated training data (6,401 and 3,143
comments), screened \taskb{} ideas on one 15\% split (472 comments), and
submitted to the validation phase on CodaBench (806 and 395 comments, labels
hidden). Afterwards we used the fixed holdouts of Section~\ref{sec:data}.

We picked each holdout's comments at random once, with a fixed random seed, and
never re-picked them. Every later experiment was tested on exactly the same
comments, so a difference between two systems cannot come from one of them
getting an easier set. Each run checks a fingerprint (a hash) of the holdout's
comment IDs and stops if it does not match. Scores from different protocols come
from different comments and are not comparable, so we name the protocol with
every number.

To tell whether one system really beats another, we resample the holdout
comments with replacement 5,000 times, score both systems on each resample, and
take the middle 95\% of the differences as a 95\% interval (a paired bootstrap;
\citealp{efron-1979-bootstrap, koehn-2004-statistical,
berg-kirkpatrick-etal-2012-empirical, dror-etal-2018-hitchhikers}).
If the whole interval is above zero, the gain is unlikely to be due to chance.

Holdout models were trained on the other 85\%. For the submission we retrained
the chosen setup on all labelled data. Those models have seen every labelled
comment, so nothing is left to test them on locally: their only score is the
one the organizers report.

\section{System Development}
\label{sec:dev}

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:prep}
The released files had been saved as UTF-8 but read back as Latin-1 (mojibake).
An emoji or a Kannada letter takes two to four bytes in UTF-8; read as Latin-1,
each byte becomes a separate meaningless character. We reverse that step
(encode as Latin-1, decode as UTF-8), let \texttt{ftfy} \citep{speer-2019-ftfy}
repair the rest, unescape
HTML entities, remove line-break tags, replace URLs and mentions with
\texttt{<url>} and \texttt{<user>}, and collapse whitespace. Shortening elongated
words (\textit{sooooo}) did not help the SVM (0.8003 against 0.8032), and we never
shorten to one letter, since a doubled letter can distinguish two Kannada words.

MuRIL's WordPiece \citep{wu-etal-2016-google, devlin-etal-2019-bert} vocabulary
has almost no emoji, and WordPiece maps the
\emph{whole} word containing an unknown character to \texttt{[UNK]}, so a slur
written against an emoji vanished with it. For MuRIL and the \taska{} SVM we
replace emoji with their English names, using the \texttt{emoji} package
\citep{kim-wurster-emoji}. This cut MuRIL's unknown-token rate from
0.79\% to 0.05\% and raised MuRIL from 0.7937 to 0.8023 on a validation split, and
an SVM from 0.8039 to 0.8103 five-fold. Gemma needs none of this: its SentencePiece
tokenizer \citep{kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece} has 262k entries and
byte-level encodings \citep{gemma-team-2026-gemma4}, so an emoji never becomes
unknown. Gemma sees emoji unchanged.

Some comments recur under different IDs (35 groups in the \taska{} training file,
12 in \taskb{}). Left in, a duplicate counts twice in the loss and can sit on both
sides of a split. We keep one copy when all copies agree and drop the group when
they disagree (10 comments in \taska{}, 6 in \taskb{}): the annotators gave the
same text two labels. Test files are not deduplicated.

\subsection{What the data told us about the language}
\label{sec:language}
Before modelling we read comments and counted things. Four observations shaped
the rest of the work.

\paragraph{Grammar fragments the vocabulary.} 74.4\% of \taska{} word types occur
once. We first blamed spelling, which back-transliteration might fix. But 65.2\%
of the once-seen words share a five-character stem with another word, and the
largest clusters are verb paradigms (106 forms of \textit{matha-}, 86 of
\textit{madid-}, 74 of \textit{matad-}, from \textit{maadu} ``do'' and
\textit{maataadu} ``speak''). Kannada is agglutinative \citep{sridhar-1990-kannada}, and script conversion
cannot merge \textit{maadtha} with \textit{maadthaare}. Simulating the merging a
transliterator performs removed 10.6\% of word types and gained 0.001, so we never
built one. The same reasoning ruled out stemming (wordpieces already split
\textit{madthare} into stem and suffixes) and stopword removal (the 60 most
frequent words include \textit{bjp}, \textit{congress} and \textit{desha},
``country'').

\paragraph{Most errors contain no slur.} 18\% of \taska{} comments contain a slur,
and an early SVM classified those with 91.6\% accuracy; 92\% of its errors were in comments with
no profanity. A held-out comment's nearest training neighbour has a median
cosine similarity of only 0.27. Features derived from word lists would
therefore mostly mark comments that are already classified correctly.

\paragraph{\taskb{} depends on the target.} On the SVM, Violence lost 103 of its 221
training comments to Gender and Others lost 282 of 447. Gendered slurs
(\textit{sule}, \textit{boli}) are directed at TV channels, cricketers and
politicians, so the lexical cue indicates Gender while the label follows the
target. The annotation convention adds a further complication: a comment with a gendered slur is labelled
Gender whoever the target is.

\paragraph{Each class has a marker.} Political, Religion and Geo-political are
decided by closed sets of names; a name list fitted inside each fold finds them
at four to nine times their base rate. Violence is a speech act: the
necessitative \textit{-beku} (``must'') on a violent verb, as in
\textit{saayisbeku} (``must be killed''), raises the share of Violence from 0.070
to 0.184. A violent word's meaning depends on its company: among 420 comments
containing one, Others is the label 27\% of the time when a media outlet is named
and 6\% when no target is named.

These observations explain many results below. Name and topic tags fire mostly
on Political and Religion, already the strongest classes, and they did not help.
What helped changed how the text is represented.

\subsection{Character n-gram SVM}
A linear SVM \citep{cortes-vapnik-1995-support, joachims-1998-text} over TF-IDF
features \citep{sparck-jones-1972-statistical}, with balanced class weights
(LIBLINEAR via scikit-learn; \citealp{fan-etal-2008-liblinear,
pedregosa-etal-2011-scikit}), was our first floor. On \taska{} five-fold macro-F1 was 0.8152 with character 2--5-grams within
word boundaries, 0.8065 with word unigrams and bigrams added and 0.7585 with
words alone: character n-grams, known to work well for abusive language
\citep{waseem-hovy-2016-hateful, mehdad-tetreault-2016-characters}, let spellings
and inflections share features (\textit{gottilla} and \textit{gotilla} share \textit{got}, \textit{til},
\textit{ill}, \textit{lla}). On \taskb{} a word and character SVM scored 0.5948.
Fourteen hyperparameter settings stayed within 0.005 of that. Calibrating its
scores with a sigmoid \citep{platt-2000-probabilistic} cost three points, because calibration pulls scores toward the class
priors and undoes the balanced weights the small classes depend on.

\subsection{MuRIL}
\label{sec:muril}
MuRIL is a BERT-base encoder \citep{devlin-etal-2019-bert} pretrained on Indian
languages, with transliterated
text, and therefore appeared well suited to this data. Fine-tuned, it scored 0.7932 five-fold on
\taska{}, below a word and character SVM on the same folds (0.8073). The tokenizer
is a likely reason: \textit{sule} becomes \textit{su}\,+\,\textit{\#\#le} and its
variant \textit{soole} becomes \textit{so}\,+\,\textit{\#\#ole}, with no pieces in
common. Only 24.9\% of \taska{} word types are one wordpiece, and the corpus uses
4.47\% of MuRIL's vocabulary.

\paragraph{Recipe.} Each choice answered something in the data
(Table~\ref{tab:muril}) and stayed unless a measurement said otherwise. CPU work
before the first GPU sweep also exposed a bug: our weight averaging (EMA) was not
bias-corrected as Adam's moments are \citep{kingma-ba-2015-adam}, so after a \taskb{} fold's 942 steps, 39\% of every evaluated
checkpoint was still the random initialization.

\paragraph{Re-initializing layers.} We began with the top \emph{two} layers
re-initialized, following \citet{zhang-etal-2021-revisiting}. On \taskb{} one
layer beat two (0.6102 against 0.6013 five-fold), with the gains in the hardest
classes (Others +0.022, Violence +0.024), and two more seeds agreed. Removing
re-initialization was clearly worse (0.584 against 0.614 for two averaged
seeds). On \taska{} one layer did not help (0.8204 against 0.8213) and two were
kept. One possible reason, untested, is that a re-initialized layer is learned
from the labels alone, and \taskb{} has half as many.

\paragraph{TAPT.} MuRIL split \taskb{} comments into 2.28 wordpieces per word, a
sign it had rarely seen this register. Masked-word TAPT on the training comments
and the external corpus of Section~\ref{sec:tapt} gained 0.029 five-fold (0.6013
against 0.5727), the only idea in our first sweep that beat both stock MuRIL and
the SVM. It also corrected a systematic weakness: the SVM predicted Violence for only 6
of 395 validation comments against a 7\% prior, the TAPT model for 24. TAPT read the
text, not the labels, of every training comment, including the fold being
scored, which favours it somewhat.

\paragraph{R-Drop.} R-Drop \citep{liang-etal-2021-rdrop} passes each batch through
the model twice with different dropout masks and adds the symmetric KL
divergence between the predictions (weight 0.5), asking for the same answer
whichever neurons are dropped. On stock MuRIL it lost (0.5614 against 0.5727
five-fold), but on TAPT MuRIL with one re-initialized layer it scored 0.6410 in
the validation phase against 0.6299 without it. One score on 395 comments has a
standard deviation of about three points, so neither result settles it; we kept
R-Drop because it was in our best submission.

\paragraph{What did not work.} \emph{Focal loss} \citep{lin-etal-2017-focal},
aimed at the 17\% of \taskb{} comments with competing topic cues, lost 0.016; we
think many ``hard'' comments here are contestable, and focal loss pushes toward
them. \emph{Vocabulary extension} cost 3--8 points: new embeddings cannot be
learned from three thousand comments. \emph{Tamil and Malayalam text} (about
58,000 comments) in TAPT cost 0.011: more text helps only if it is the same kind
of text. An \emph{auxiliary violence head} cost 0.006. \emph{Other encoders}:
with almost no Kannada script in the corpus, Latin-script models seemed worth
trying, but XLM-R \citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised}, HingRoBERTa
\citep{nayak-joshi-2022-l3cube} and an abuse-tuned MuRIL \citep{das-etal-2022-data}
all scored below MuRIL,
and mDeBERTa \citep{he-etal-2023-debertav3} collapsed. \emph{Decision rules}
fitted on predictions lost their nested checks: four \taskb{} models blended to
0.6032 with weights searched on the scored rows, but 0.5925 with weights refitted
out of fold, below the best member (0.5979). \emph{Synthetic comments} added
+0.003, inside noise, and a linear model told them from real ones at an AUC of
0.942, separating them by the generator's lexical choices.

\paragraph{\taska{} blends.} Blending MuRIL with the SVM added 0.016 nested five-fold
(0.8230 against 0.8073), but scored 0.7890 in the validation phase, below MuRIL
alone (0.8163). Refitting both on all training data gave 0.8187, and a variant
with one re-initialized layer tied at 0.8188. A frozen MuRIL with an RBF SVM
scored about 0.71, and a binary task shows why such a model cannot help a blend:
on the 184 of 806 comments where it disagreed with our best system it was right
24\% of the time, so overruling the stronger model would be wrong three times in
four. A character-only SVM as a third member gave 0.8250 nested, against 0.8184
for the best single model. For Table~\ref{tab:results} the \taska{} reference is
a 0.57/0.43 SVM and MuRIL blend; the \taskb{} reference retrains the 0.6410
recipe (eight epochs of TAPT, six of fine-tuning, five seeds) on the holdout
split.

\subsection{Translation into English}
The organizers' published pipeline\footnote{\texttt{github.com/shankarb14/SLM-Impact}}
transliterates comments into Kannada script with IndicXlit
\citep{madhani-etal-2023-aksharantar} and translates them with IndicTrans2
\citep{gala-etal-2023-indictrans2}. Translation would remove the spelling problem
and open English abuse models, so we evaluated it, first checking whether slurs
survived translation, with a slur list that tolerates spelling variants (phonetic skeletons,
bounded edit distance, compound splitting). IndicTrans2 failed to install on
Kaggle, so we used Sarvam-Translate\footnote{\texttt{sarvamai/sarvam-translate}}
and fine-tuned hateBERT \citep{caselli-etal-2021-hatebert} on the output. Only
1\% of 300 slur-bearing comments kept an abusive term: outputs either echoed the
input or were fluent and unrelated (\textit{Paapa how she matadtale with avar
tayi} became ``How she killed her uncle by eating him''). hateBERT scored 0.7275
on the \taska{} holdout, 0.053 below MuRIL ([$-0.078$, $-0.027$]), and lowered
every blend it joined. The spelling-tolerant slur list itself gave the SVM a small,
unconfirmed +0.003.

\subsection{Choosing a large language model}
TAPT and layer re-initialization both changed the encoder's weights, which we
took as a sign that the representation was the bottleneck. To choose a decoder
without labels, we measured how well each model predicts 600 unlabelled training
comments in bits per character, which unlike per-token perplexity is comparable
across tokenizers: Gemma-4-12B 3.011, Sarvam-1\footnote{\texttt{sarvamai/sarvam-1}}
3.081, Qwen3-8B-Base \citep{yang-etal-2025-qwen3} 3.192, Gemma-4-E4B 4.295. On the
\taskb{} holdout split Sarvam-1 (2B) scored 0.5567, 0.058 below MuRIL, while two
averaged Gemma-4-12B models scored 0.6792, 0.064 above it. Gemma was chosen by a
screen that saw no labels, so this gain does not result from selecting the best
of several fine-tuned candidates.

\section{Final System}
\label{sec:final}

\subsection{Gemma-4-12B classifier}
\paragraph{Why 4-bit quantization.} We trained on Kaggle's free GPUs: two NVIDIA
T4 cards per session, about 15\,GB each, sessions of at most 12 hours and about
30 GPU-hours a week. A 12B model in 16-bit floats needs about 24\,GB for its
weights alone. In 4 bits the weights take about a quarter of that. We use QLoRA
\citep{dettmers-etal-2023-qlora}: the backbone is frozen in 4-bit NF4 with double
quantization, and only LoRA adapters \citep{hu-etal-2022-lora} (rank 16,
$\alpha=32$, dropout 0.05, on all attention and MLP projections) and the head are
trained, with gradient checkpointing \citep{chen-etal-2016-training}, in PyTorch,
Transformers, PEFT and bitsandbytes \citep{paszke-etal-2019-pytorch,
wolf-etal-2020-transformers, mangrulkar-etal-2022-peft, dettmers-etal-2022-llmint8}.
Our \taskb{} holdout fits peaked at 10.0\,GB. Gemma-4 26B and 31B do not
fit a T4 even in 4 bits.

\paragraph{Gemma as an encoder.} Each comment goes into a short prompt ending in a
question: \texttt{Kannada-English (romanized) YouTube comment: \{comment\}}, a line
break, and \texttt{Is this hate speech (Hate or Non-Hate)?:}; the \taskb{} prompt
calls the comment hateful and lists the six categories. In a decoder only the
last position has seen the whole input, so we classify the final-layer hidden
state $h_i$ of the last non-padding token:
$p_i = \mathrm{softmax}(W\,\mathrm{Dropout}(h_i) + b)$, with $W, b$ trained from
scratch and dropout 0.1 \citep{srivastava-etal-2014-dropout}. A head gives a probability per class, which our
ensembles average. The other settings (AdamW, \citealp{loshchilov-hutter-2019-decoupled}; weight
decay 0.01) and the reason for each:

Re-initialization and emoji replacement were MuRIL fixes and were not carried
over: Gemma's tokenizer handles emoji, and LoRA leaves the pretrained weights
frozen. R-Drop (weight 0.5) is used in some members. Four \taska{} fits on all
7,193 comments, two at a time on two GPUs, took 5.6 hours without TAPT and 7.6
with it. In four runs a model collapsed onto one class, a known failure of fine-tuning
on small data \citep{dodge-etal-2020-fine, mosbach-etal-2021-stability}, first in
our first Gemma run, where a holdout model predicted Non-Hate for everything. Since then, after
epoch 1 the model predicts 400 training comments, and if more than 95\% go to one
class with a prior under 90\%, the run restarts with a new seed. No collapsed
model is in any ensemble.

\subsection{Task-adaptive pretraining}
\label{sec:tapt}
For Gemma, TAPT is next-token LoRA training on the training comments, without
labels, plus 3,247 Kannada YouTube comments from the DravidianLangTech-2021
offensive-language data \citep{hande-etal-2020-kancmd,
chakravarthi-etal-2021-findings-shared}, after
removing any that match a HASTIKA comment. That corpus is romanized like HASTIKA,
although its card says Kannada script. Holdout and test comments are never used.
The classifier starts from the TAPT adapter. \taska{} uses one TAPT epoch (the
language-model loss fell from 7.5 to 5.6), \taskb{}, with half the data, two. The
logits over Gemma's 262k-word vocabulary do not fit beside the model, so we apply
the frozen output layer to 16 positions at a time on a detached copy of the
hidden states, accumulate their gradient, and run one backward pass through the
decoder, with the same gradient as the unchunked loss. Each micro-batch has one
128-token sequence, with 16 accumulation steps. \taska{} TAPT took 125 minutes,
peaking at 10.4\,GB.

\subsection{Ensembles}
\label{sec:ensembles}
A submitted system adds up its members' class probabilities with equal weight
and predicts the class with the highest total; no weights or thresholds were
tuned, so the holdout had no say in how members combine. Members come in
\emph{groups}: one TAPT run, producing one adapter, followed by two or four
classifiers that start from it with different seeds. Classifiers in a group share
an adapter and make similar mistakes; classifiers from different groups disagree
more, which is what averaging needs \citep{dietterich-2000-ensemble,
lakshminarayanan-etal-2017-simple}. Some groups use R-Drop, whose models were
not better alone but raised the average when added (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
Our rule, fixed in advance, was to average every TAPT model we had trained on all
labelled data:

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}


\paragraph{Official results.} The organizers report 0.8250 macro-F1 (accuracy
0.8784) on \taska{} and 0.7367 (0.7879) on \taskb{} (Table~\ref{tab:results}).
These are their figures; without test labels we cannot recompute them. Our
holdout macro-F1 on \taska{} was higher than the reported score: 0.8721 for the
best ensemble, 0.047 above it, and every Gemma model and ensemble, single models
included, scored above it. On \taskb{} the reported score is 0.031 above our best
holdout result. We expected both submissions to match our best holdouts: their
models saw about 18\% more data and the ensembles are larger. The \taskb{} score
and the \taska{} accuracy are above every holdout figure we recorded, and on the
\taska{} holdout independent TAPT runs agreed (pairs and fours 0.8619--0.8721,
single models 0.848--0.861). We cannot tell why the \taska{} macro-F1 is lower.
Training is not deterministic and the submitted models are new runs; our holdout
scores are optimistic, since we chose configurations on them; and the test
comments may differ in topic or class balance.

\paragraph{Gemma against the baselines.} One Gemma model beat the \taska{} SVM by
0.043 ([0.021, 0.067]); two Gemma models beat \taskb{} MuRIL by 0.064 ([0.024,
0.105]). The old systems no longer helped: a 50/50 blend with the SVM scored
0.8471 on \taska{}, below Gemma alone, and adding MuRIL on \taskb{} gave 0.6825
against 0.6792. Four epochs helped \taskb{}, mostly Violence (0.483 against 0.410;
unpaired runs), and hurt \taska{} (0.8313 against 0.8610 in one run), whose larger
data peaked by epoch two or three.

\paragraph{TAPT.} At equal size, two TAPT models from two TAPT runs scored 0.8619
and 0.8675 on \taska{}, against 0.8610 without TAPT; single TAPT models averaged
0.8519 over four seeds against 0.8483 over three. The gain grew with ensemble
size: four TAPT models scored 0.8702, three without TAPT 0.8582 (no paired
interval), and two R-Drop models added to a TAPT pair gave 0.8721 (+0.010, [0.000,
0.020]). We cannot separate TAPT's gain from that of averaging more models. On
\taskb{} two TAPT runs differing only in seed scored 0.6741 and 0.7056, either
side of the 0.6872 without TAPT, a seed effect familiar from fine-tuning
\citep{dodge-etal-2020-fine}; one Geo-political comment is 3.2\% of that
class's recall here. In the first run, R-Drop models scored 0.029 above plain ones
([$-0.004$, 0.062]) and all four together 0.023 above the plain pair ([0.004,
0.042]). Two runs cannot show whether TAPT helps \taskb{}; we submitted TAPT
ensembles because they gave the best holdout score on both tasks and a second
\taska{} TAPT run repeated the first's gain.

\paragraph{What did not help on Gemma.} On \taskb{} without TAPT, the organizers'
category definitions in the prompt tied (0.6840 against 0.6830), mixing both
prompts gave +0.005 ([$-0.018$, 0.027]), square-root class weights lost 0.004
(their higher accuracy came from the large classes), and R-Drop changed
macro-F1 by $-0.002$ ([$-0.027$, 0.023]): each R-Drop model beat its plain twin,
but their average did not, consistent with R-Drop increasing agreement between
models.

\paragraph{Per class.} For the best \taskb{} pair (0.7056), Violence (0.532) and
Others (0.559) are weakest, Political (0.824) and Gender (0.821) strongest: the
classes Section~\ref{sec:language} predicts to be hard are those defined by the
target and the act, not by names.

\section{Discussion}
Replacing the baseline with Gemma is the only comparison whose bootstrap interval
lies above zero on both tasks, and the unlabelled screen chose Gemma before any
fine-tuning. Little was tuned on the holdout: equal weights, no thresholds, and
only the \taskb{} epoch count, TAPT and the R-Drop members chosen by comparison.
Most of what helped changed the representation: character n-grams, TAPT,
re-initialized layers and Gemma. Features built from word lists mostly marked
comments already classified correctly; the remaining errors are in comments
without profanity (\taska{}) and in classes defined by the target (\taskb{}),
which need a model that reads the whole comment. We cannot say what makes Gemma
better than MuRIL, since size, pretraining data, tokenizer and fine-tuning all
changed at once.

Of 18 averages of two or more same-recipe models, 13 beat their best member (by
0.002--0.026) and five fell between their members. Averaging in weaker models
(four-epoch \taska{} models, the SVM, MuRIL) did not help. TAPT's \taska{} gains
appear mainly in ensembles, perhaps because TAPT decorrelates errors; we have not
measured this.

The submitted ensembles were limited by the available compute, and their design
was not optimized; their size reflects the number of TAPT runs that could be completed. More seeds, more TAPT runs and
a second model family would likely have helped. Appendix~\ref{app:unfinished}
lists what we prepared but did not finish, including Qwen3-8B.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
We release our code, both recipes and a reproduction package\footnote{The
branch \texttt{reproduce} of our repository contains only what is needed to rebuild
the submitted systems:
\url{https://github.com/Aaryan127/ProjectMANAS-HastikaICON2026/tree/reproduce}.}. A system is
rebuilt group by group. Each group (one TAPT run and its classifiers) is one
Kaggle session on two T4 GPUs, TAPT first and then the classifiers two at a
time, in 4.5 to 7.6 hours: about 30 GPU-hours for \taska{} and 20 for \taskb{}.
One notebook per session needs only the organizer files and the external
corpus; a final CPU notebook averages the members and writes
the submission file. A script does the same on a local GPU and resumes after an
interruption. Every data file is checked against a recorded checksum and each
holdout against its fingerprint. Since a rerun cannot be scored without test
labels, a holdout recipe retrains the same groups on the 85\% split and reports
each group's score beside the original run of that group. A rerun will not match
exactly: GPU training is not deterministic, the base-model revision and some
replacement seeds were not recorded, and removing external comments that match
test comments changes one \taska{} TAPT comment. A smoke test runs every stage
on a small sample in about 30 minutes, including reloading a saved model and
checking that it reproduces its predictions; the package's orchestration is also
tested on a CPU.

\section{Conclusion}
Analysis of the data identified the main challenges: agglutinative
morphology, unstable spelling, slurs whose category depends on their target, and
grammatical markers of violent intent. A 4-bit Gemma-4-12B with LoRA and a
classification head, the largest model that fits a single T4 GPU, beat our
best SVM by 0.043 on \taska{} and our best MuRIL system by 0.064 on \taskb{}, and
TAPT ensembles reached 0.8721 and 0.7056 on our holdouts. For our submissions,
12 and 8 TAPT models trained on all labelled data, the organizers report 0.8250
and 0.7367 macro-F1.

\section*{Limitations}
We had no test labels, so apart from the official scores every number here comes
from labelled released data. We compared more than twenty configurations on the
same holdouts, so the best scores are likely optimistic, and the holdouts are
small: two runs of one \taskb{} configuration differed by 0.03. Most comparisons in
Table~\ref{tab:dev} are single runs without intervals, and several settings in
Table~\ref{tab:muril} and Section~\ref{sec:final} rest on reasoning, without
ablation. We fine-tuned only two decoders. Reproduction needs the Gemma-4-12B
weights, transformers 5.16.1, PEFT 0.20.0 and the bitsandbytes 4-bit kernels, and
may not match exactly (Section~\ref{sec:repro}). For some runs we kept only logs,
not weights or probabilities.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Labels were assigned to single comments without their thread or video, and some
comments are hard to judge without that context. We dropped the identical
comments that carry conflicting labels (10 in \taska{}, 6 in \taskb{}); the rest
of the label subjectivity remains. A false positive can suppress legitimate
speech such as political criticism, and a false negative leaves abuse in place.
Hate-speech classifiers can also be biased against particular groups and
dialects \citep{sap-etal-2019-risk}.
Accuracy varies by class, from 0.53 F1 for Violence to 0.82 for Political on our
best \taskb{} pair. The system should not moderate without human review; Kannada
speakers should review its errors, and it should be tested on newer comments, as
spelling and topics change. The external TAPT comments are released under CC BY
4.0.

\section{Development History}
\label{app:timeline}
This appendix summarizes the order in which approaches were developed and the
evidence that motivated each step, including negative results.

\paragraph{Baselines.} The initial plan used XLM-R, which remained at the
all-one-class solution for two epochs and reached 0.716 in a CPU run. MuRIL
reached 0.8023 on a validation split with emoji replaced by their names, while a
TF-IDF SVM reached 0.8103 five-fold, establishing the SVM as the floor for all
later \taska{} systems. A set of 150 synthetic comments was separable from real
comments at an AUC of 0.94 and was not pursued.

\paragraph{Data-processing interventions.} After deduplication, construction of
the external corpus and the CPU analysis of Section~\ref{sec:language}, two
sweeps on \taskb{} (8.4 and 8.9 hours) evaluated corpus mixing, vocabulary
extension, an auxiliary violence head and alternative encoders. Only TAPT
improved on stock MuRIL. The subsequent work therefore concentrated on the
representation.

\paragraph{Improving the MuRIL recipe.} TAPT MuRIL with one re-initialized layer
and R-Drop reached 0.6410 in the \taskb{} validation phase, the best result
before Gemma. Further candidates were derived from the data analysis: entity
and topic tags, sentiment and address features, stopword removal, stemming and
lemmatization, removal of redundant Gender comments, and a confidence threshold
for Violence and Others. The tags lowered the SVM by 0.007; stopword removal
and stemming were ruled out by the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:language}; and
the threshold was developed into a context-aware decoding rule that gained 2.9
points on the SVM but was not evaluated on MuRIL. On \taska{}, an SVM and MuRIL
blend that scored 0.8233 out of fold scored 0.7890 in the validation phase,
which motivated refitting both members on all training data (0.8187).

\paragraph{Translation.} Following the organizers' pipeline, comments were
translated into English after a slur-survival check. Only 1\% of slurs
survived, and hateBERT on the translations scored 0.053 below MuRIL, so the
approach was discontinued in favour of reading the romanized text directly with
a larger model.

\paragraph{Decoder language models.} 4-bit quantization made a 12B model
trainable on a T4 GPU. Gemma-4-12B ranked first in the unlabelled screen and
produced the largest gains of the project, 0.064 on \taskb{} and 0.043 on
\taska{}. One \taska{} seed collapsed to a single class, which led to the
collapse guard of Section~\ref{sec:final}. R-Drop improved individual Gemma
models but not their average. TAPT on Gemma at first exceeded GPU memory; the
fixes are described in Section~\ref{sec:tapt}.

\paragraph{TAPT ensembles.} Four TAPT models reached 0.8702 on the \taska{}
holdout, and 0.8721 with two R-Drop models added. On \taskb{}, two TAPT runs
differing only in seed scored 0.6741 and 0.7056. Because single configurations
varied by about three points with the seed, further tuning on the holdout was
avoided in favour of a fixed rule: average every independently trained TAPT
model (Section~\ref{sec:ensembles}), with three additional TAPT runs per task.
